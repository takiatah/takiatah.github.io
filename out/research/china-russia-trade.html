China–Russia Trade Relations and Risks

How has risk evolved and redistributed in China and Russia trade, and how are they affected by geopolitical disruptions? This dashboard brings together geopolitical, concentration, dependency, transport, and economic risks. The goal is to put all of these together to create an overall risk framework that can be used on any trade partners.

Dashboard Overview

China and Russia has been chosen as the case study because trade between them has been rapidly increasing, especially after the Russian annexation of Crimea in February 2014 and Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. These events drastically changed global supply chains and trade dynamics, as Russia was forced to reduce trade with Western countries and rely more on China. While some risks were reduced, new risks were introduced. The research concentrates on how trade and risk factors are affected by these disputions. The dashboard focuses on 5 risk areas: geopolitical, concentration, dependency, transport, and economic risks.

Geopolitical Risk

The country-specific Geopolitical Risk (GPR) Index measures the share of newspaper articles that discuss adverse geopolitical events and mention a given country. Higher values mean more coverage of geopolitical tension involving that country. Both countries' geopilitical risk spikes from February 2022 to March 2022, right after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Risk also increases in Febrary 2014.

Interpret with care: the index reflects English-language newspaper coverage, so it measures perceived risk rather than conflict events directly.

Trade Concentration

How dependent is each side on a few product categories? Concentration is summarized by each of the top 10 category’s share of reported exports for each country and the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI). A high HHI indicates exports are more concentrated on a smaller number of categories. Russia has much higher HHI because of its high reliance on mineral fuels, while China's trade is much more spread out. This introduces a concentration risk for Russia. HHI for Russia is heavily influenced by its mineral fuel exports, and it drops in 2020, possibly due to the decrease of fuel exports during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Sources, methodology, and data limitations

Commodity-level export data for 2010–2014 are sourced from UN Comtrade. Data for 2015–2026 are sourced from the General Administration of Customs of China.

Estimated observations: Values from October 2012 through December 2014, inclusive, are estimated based on annual values rather than observed monthly values. Interpret month-to-month changes during this period with caution.

Each commodity line represents the corresponding category's share of reported exports, using the precomputed _CON columns in the country-specific datasets. The charts do not recalculate these shares.

The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) series uses the precomputed CHN_HHI and RUS_HHI columns in main_data.csv. HHI measures overall concentration across categories and is distinct from the share of any individual commodity.

HS codes identify commodity categories. The categories shown are the current top ten categories for each country, so they should not necessarily be interpreted as the historical top ten in every month.

Source and methodology

Source: International Monetary Fund (IMF), IMF Data.

Series shown: RUS_IMP_DEP, RUS_EXP_DEP, CHN_IMP_DEP, and CHN_EXP_DEP. These monthly indicators measure the relative importance of bilateral trade between China and Russia for each country's imports and exports.

Import dependency and export dependency are calculated separately using bilateral trade flows relative to the corresponding country's total trade flows. Values are displayed as percentages.

Interpret with care: trade dependency measures the relative importance of a trading relationship, not the economic cost of a disruption or the availability of alternative suppliers and markets.
